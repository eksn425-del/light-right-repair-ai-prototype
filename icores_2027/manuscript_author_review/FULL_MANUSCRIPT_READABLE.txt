Auditable Solar-Access Decision Support through Exact
    Blocker Reuse and Containment-Constrained
                     Translation



       Abstract. Early-stage solar-access decisions require efficient comparison of in-
       terventions and reliable translation of simplified rules into geometry. We study
       a bounded decision-support workflow that selects two of 36 eligible building
       groups in a 45-building canonical model, giving 630 uniform-height-reduction
       candidates. Building-level occlusion reuse evaluates every candidate with the
       same ray–footprint primitive as repeated intersection, while a separate contain-
       ment audit checks translated stepback solids. Across three matched single-thread
       repeats, full-universe median runtimes are 11,608.183 s for repeated intersection
       and 24.037 s for reuse, including precomputation, with equivalent pointwise ex-
       posure, metrics, scores, ranks and Pareto results. This 482.927-fold ratio applies
       to these two implementations, not to arbitrary solar-analysis software. Frozen
       same-site comparison cohorts of 23 and 20 candidates show strong aggregate
       agreement with archived Honeybee/Radiance results; the 20-candidate low-sun-
       area mean absolute error is 0.45 m2 , arising from one 9 m2 grid-cell discrepancy.
       Six controlled historical replays identify outside-footprint walls as the cause of
       adverse sunlight changes. Four separately simulated contained corrections re-
       move those adverse events on the fixed grid and rays. The canonical geometry
       remains a component-bounding-box approximation with overlapping footprints.
       The contribution is an auditable computational screening and translation work-
       flow, conditional on that model, rather than as-built validation or an automatically
       approved design. Drafting and editing assistance is disclosed for this abstract and
       the paper [6].

1   Introduction
Solar access is both an environmental performance concern and a constraint on changes
to urban form.1 A decision-support system must make the comparison of alternatives
computationally manageable without obscuring what its model actually represents. Ur-
ban solar analysis encompasses different quantities, including irradiation, illuminance
and duration of direct exposure; these quantities serve different decisions and should
not be treated as interchangeable [1,2]. Here the outcome is sampled outdoor direct-sun
duration on a fixed receiving grid. It is neither an indoor daylight metric nor a prediction
of annual energy production.
    Two practical difficulties motivate this work. First, related candidate interventions
repeat much of the same occlusion calculation. When only two building heights change,

recomputing all geometric intersections for every candidate discards reusable informa-
tion. Second, an attractive result in a simplified screening model does not guarantee that
a subsequently generated design solid implements the intended intervention. A nomi-
nally subtractive stepback can inadvertently add material outside the original footprint.
Ranking such an invalid translation more carefully does not repair its physical meaning.
    We examine these difficulties together in a bounded case study. The decision universe
contains all 630 pairs from 36 eligible building groups within a 45-building canonical
model. Eligibility is an input constraint for research, not permission to modify a property.
Each pair receives a prescribed uniform-height reduction and is evaluated by low-sun
grid area, mean direct-sun duration, a nominal volume proxy and newly low-sun grid
area. A fixed weighted score expresses preferences; an area–volume Pareto analysis
offers a complementary view. Translated stepback geometries are evaluated separately
rather than assumed equivalent to the screening rules.
    The study addresses three questions. (1) What efficiency gain does building-level
blocker reuse deliver against repeated intersection when both implementations use the
same primitive, inputs, single-thread execution and output checks? (2) How closely
do frozen screening aggregates agree with archived Honeybee/Radiance aggregates
within two preserved same-site review cohorts? (3) Can the adverse exposure changes
in historical translations be explained by actual ray hits and removed by separately
evaluated contained geometry? These questions distinguish implementation efficiency,
aggregate agreement and translation validity. None alone establishes real-site accuracy.
    The contributions are a bounded formulation with exact blocker reuse, a four-scale
comparison including precomputation, and a controlled translation audit retaining nega-
tive historical outcomes and evaluating four corrections. Explicit metric, tie and volume
semantics connect these results to their limitations. The contribution is an auditable
application-specific workflow, not a learned model or a new general optimization algo-
rithm.


2   Related Work and Positioning
Urban solar-performance assessment has long considered the interaction between built
form and the availability of sunlight and daylight.2 Compagnon [1] evaluates solar and
daylight availability in urban fabric through surface-based performance analysis. The
taxonomy of Czachura et al. [2] helps distinguish the metrics used in urban planning. This
distinction matters here: a threshold on sampled direct-sun hours cannot be substituted
for irradiation, daylight autonomy or a jurisdiction-specific compliance test.
    Simulation-based design-support methods already connect changes in building form
to environmental objectives. Konis et al. [5] present an optimization framework for early-
stage passive-design support. De Luca et al. [3] develop a reverse solar-envelope form-
finding method that can accommodate regulatory frameworks. Our study has a narrower
search space: it exhaustively ranks a fixed set of two-building height-reduction rules,
then treats stepback generation as a separate, fallible translation. We do not benchmark
against those systems or infer that exhaustive enumeration outperforms their broader
form-finding methods.

4.2   Computational Cost and Accounting
Let 𝐵, 𝑃, 𝑇, 𝑀 be the building, receiver, time-sample and candidate counts, and let
𝐺 denote the cost of a ray–footprint intersection. Repeated candidate intersection has
work of order 𝑂 (𝑀 𝐵𝑃𝑇𝐺) before early exits. Reuse requires 𝑂 (𝐵𝑃𝑇𝐺) preparation
and 𝑂 (𝑀 𝑃𝑇) candidate updates and metrics. Polygon complexity and possible indexing
affect 𝐺; it is not a universal constant. Score sorting adds 𝑂 (𝑀 log 𝑀) and the present
direct Pareto scan adds 𝑂 (𝑀 2 ) comparisons.
     Two byte-valued blocker arrays require 2𝐵𝑃𝑇 bytes. At the present dimensions this
is 2,924,640 bytes, approximately 2.79 MiB. This analytic storage count excludes geom-
etry, solar data, integer count arrays, temporaries, indexes and Python overhead. It is not
measured peak process memory. Input preparation, precomputation, candidate process-
ing and output costs must be included consistently when evaluating actual efficiency.
     We therefore compare two specific implementations: A repeats the primitive for each
candidate, retaining first-blocker short-circuiting; B precomputes building blockers and
reuses them. Neither implementation uses a spatial index in this experiment. B’s end-
to-end runtime includes independent precomputation for each repeat. A warm-cache
update-only time would answer a different question and is not used as the denominator
of the reported speedup.

4.3   Containment as a Physical Invariant
Let Ω0 be the baseline opaque solid and Ω𝑐 a translated candidate solid. A genuine
subtraction satisfies Ω𝑐 ⊆ Ω0 . For fixed receiving points and solar rays, a ray unob-
structed by Ω0 cannot become obstructed by a subset of Ω0 . Thus containment implies
nondecreasing sampled direct-sun exposure. An adverse event under these assumptions
requires examination of geometry, ray mapping, receiving-point mapping or computa-
tion; it cannot simply be justified as an unavoidable trade-off of subtraction.
    Historical stepback generation is therefore audited independently of score ranking.
The audit checks containment of generated solids, preservation of expected faces, actual
ray intersections for adverse visibility events, and whole-scene union volumes. The
corrected generator constrains the translated geometry to the original solid footprint and
is evaluated in separate runs. Containment is not volume matching: clipping an outside
region may change the amount removed, and overlapping building footprints make per-
building sums differ from scene-union differences. We preserve both the original and
corrected outputs rather than replacing the history with the favorable result.
5     Experimental Protocol

5.1   Evidence Layers and Frozen Inputs

Three evidence layers are kept separate.5 The matched implementation experiment uses
the canonical real-case input and the complete fixed candidate order. The same-metric
agreement study recomputes statistics from frozen archived aggregates for a curated
cohort of 23 and a separately preserved coverage cohort of 20. Finally, controlled
Honeybee/Radiance replays revisit a baseline and five historical translations, followed
by four corrected scenes. The original 20-candidate sample is neither reselected nor
rerun in the correction experiment.
    The coverage cohort was selected for score-stratum, volume and identifier coverage
and held out from curated review. It is an outcome-blind same-site coverage sample,
not a probability sample or an independent site. Archived timestamps report freezing
before its Honeybee execution; a file hash verifies unchanged bytes but does not by itself
prove that chronology. We use descriptive agreement measures without a population-
generalization claim.
    A pre-benchmark amendment repaired a normalization-source hash mismatch, pin-
ning the actual committed bytes and an independent frozen reference. All 630 candidate
identities and metrics agreed within existing tolerance. The original failed check was
retained; score bounds were not adapted to outcomes.


5.2   Matched Runtime and Equivalence

The runtime experiment uses nested fixed prefixes of 5, 20, 60 and 630 candidates,
with three measured A/B repeats at each size. Warm-up and pilot runs are excluded
from the reported medians. Each comparison fixes geometry, ordered receivers, solar
vectors, tolerance, candidate order, score bounds and phase accounting. Equivalence
is checked for pointwise duration, 𝐴/𝐻/𝑉/𝑁, scores, ranks and Pareto membership
before reporting runtime ratios. The 630-candidate A runs were completed; their times
are measured rather than extrapolated.
    The environment is Windows 11, Python 3.12.2, NumPy 2.4.6, Shapely 2.1.2 with
GEOS 3.13.1, and pvlib 0.15.2. The processor is recorded as AMD64 Family 25 Model
80 Stepping 0, with 16 logical CPUs available. The experiment uses one process and
one thread; common numerical-library thread environment variables are fixed to one.
Elapsed time is measured with time.perf_counter. The record does not supply a veri-
fied commercial CPU model name, and unavailable Windows resident-set measurements
are left unknown rather than reported as zero.

5.4   Controlled Translation Replays
Six controlled replays comprise the baseline and five historical translated pairs: C9/C24,
C9/C15, C9/C13, C13/C24 and C6/C33. Their ordered receiver grid, solar sources, time
columns and archived scene identity are checked. New runs use one CPU and one
worker. All six reproduce their historical pointwise visibility matrices exactly. Four
contained corrections are then simulated separately. C9/C13 was already contained and
is not simulated again as a correction; its unchanged value is reused only for comparison.
There are therefore ten new scene runs, not eleven, and no full 630-scene Honeybee
experiment.


6     Results
6.1   Measured Reuse Efficiency
All 12 measured scale/repeat comparisons pass pointwise and aggregate equivalence
checks.6 Table 2 and Fig. 1 report the scale-level medians. At 630 candidates, repeated
intersection takes 11,608.183 s and reuse takes 24.037 s including precomputation; their
ratio is 482.927. The relative benefit increases with the candidate count as repeated
geometry work accumulates while preparation is amortized.
    This is an implementation comparison, not a general solver comparison. In partic-
ular, the repeated-intersection implementation is not a spatial-index baseline, and the
experiment does not time equivalent Honeybee scenes against B. Historical screening
and Honeybee timings with different machines, solver semantics or worker counts are
excluded from the ratio. The result supports the benefit of reuse in the measured setting,
while leaving the benefit relative to other optimized implementations unmeasured.

6.2   Agreement within Preserved Cohorts
Table 3 separates the two cohorts and the three compared metrics. For low-sun-area
reduction, the curated cohort has Spearman 𝜌 = 0.999258 and the coverage cohort
𝜌 = 0.997668. In the latter, 19 candidates agree exactly and one differs by 9 m2 ,
yielding MAE 0.45 m2 . This is an average candidate-aggregate error on a grid with 9 m2
cell weights; it is not sub-grid spatial precision. Figure 2 visualizes the same frozen
aggregate comparison.
    Coverage-cohort score ranks agree perfectly, but the score values do not: MAE is
0.002139 and only 9 of 20 scores agree exactly. Moreover, 𝑉 is shared between routes
and 𝑁 is constant. The score comparison cannot validate four independent performance
criteria, and correlation one cannot be read as 100% predictive accuracy. The exposure
routes also have different effective solar vectors. Their high aggregate agreement is
useful evidence for this frozen screening task, with those qualifications.
    Tie handling materially affects the top-five 𝐴 comparison within the 20-candidate
coverage cohort. Here overlap is divided by five for exact-five sets. The retained legacy
source-order result of 0.8 becomes 1.0 under the specified natural-pair exact-five rule,
while including all boundary ties produces sets of six and seven candidates. This is a
change in set definition, not an improvement in the physical model. We consequently
avoid presenting a single top-five overlap as a universal selection guarantee. Likewise,
an older correlation that compared score ranks to area ranks is not compared to the
present same-metric correlations as evidence of progress.

7     Discussion and Limitations
7.1   Implications for Decision Support
Reuse makes exhaustive comparison inexpensive after building contributions have been
prepared.7 The bounded universe exposes all candidates, fixed normalization bounds
and the area–volume trade-off. Altering the rule, geometry or solar protocol requires
new blocker contributions and renewed checks.
    The translation result is equally important for decision support. A scoring system
can be internally consistent while a downstream geometric representation violates its
physical premise. Containment supplies an inexpensive conceptual invariant, and ray-
level evidence distinguishes an actual added obstruction from a speculative explanation.
Preserving the failed translation alongside the correction prevents an apparent design
benefit from erasing the diagnostic evidence. It also makes clear that ranking and
translation are separate stages with different acceptance criteria.
    The workflow supports shortlisting under a declared model, followed by separate
geometric and performance review. Cost, ownership, safety, constructability and regu-
latory acceptability remain external decisions. The weights are not a validated social
preference model; professional use requires verified geometry and stakeholder-specific
criteria.

7.2   Threats to Validity
Canonical geometry. The DXF component-bounding-box unions, shared components,
overlapping footprints and OBJ disagreements are the most important external-validity
limits. Reproducing a canonical model across implementations cannot validate its as-
built fidelity. Correcting those boundaries would define a new input version and require
an explicit assessment of affected results. Current cohort agreement must not be trans-
ferred to that new geometry without evidence.

Restricted physical scope. A single day, 16 samples, a 3 m grid, a 0.1 m receiving
height and an assumed north orientation delimit the reported performance. Threshold
counts can change discontinuously near 3 h. The constant WEA carrier is not measured
meteorology, and the study does not validate indoor illuminance, glare, annual weather,
energy production or regulatory compliance. The distinction between saved pvlib rays
and archived Ladybug rays remains part of the cross-route comparison rather than being
suppressed to imply identical astronomical inputs.

Comparison design. The matched timing comparison controls the geometric primitive
and single-thread execution, but covers only one canonical model and two implementa-
tions without spatial indexing. Three repeats quantify the recorded execution conditions;
they do not characterize hardware populations or arbitrary polygon complexity. We do
not claim a measured memory reduction from the analytic blocker-array size or unavail-
able RSS values. A stronger efficiency comparison would include indexed and compiled
baselines with the same accounting contract.

Cohorts and metrics. Curated review and outcome-blind coverage serve different pur-
poses and remain separate. Neither is a random multi-site sample. Shared volume inputs,
constant 𝑁, discretized area, ties and preference weights constrain the interpretation of
rank agreement. Candidate-level aggregate errors can mask spatial differences. The
frozen 20 were not selectively rerun after the translation audit because no shared re-
ceiver, temporal-parser or missing-face defect was found in the actual scenes. A newly
demonstrated shared defect would require an affected-scope review rather than selective
retention of favorable outputs.

Correction scope. Four corrected translations remove the observed adverse events under
fixed rays and receivers. This supports the tested containment correction on this canonical
model, not a guarantee for arbitrary imported meshes. Containment does not ensure
equal volume, structural feasibility or a preferred design. The nominal 𝑉 proxy should
be replaced or supplemented by an appropriate audited quantity if the decision requires
physical removal costs.

7.3   Reproducibility and Responsible Release
The evidence separates measured runtime, newly executed scene replays, frozen-table
statistical recomputation and historical reused results. Run identifiers, source, config-
uration and input hashes, raw timing repeats, aggregate tables, equivalence checks,
correction ledgers and figure-source manifests are retained. The final integrated test
record contains 112 passing tests; this supports software regression coverage, not phys-
ical ground truth. Failures and amendments remain part of the record rather than being
overwritten.
    Raw CAD, DXF, SKP and OBJ inputs, receiver coordinates, WEA carriers, Honey-
bee models and Radiance intermediate data remain restricted. Figures in this manuscript
show aggregate evidence without site geometry or receiver coordinates. No public arti-
fact release or unrestricted replication right is asserted. Any external evidence package
must be reviewed for rights and anonymity before distribution. This restriction limit-
s independent reproduction of the site-specific experiment and should remain visible
alongside the available aggregate and algorithmic detail.


8     Conclusion
A bounded solar-access screening problem can reuse exact building-level occlusion
contributions while keeping its preference model and geometric limitations explicit.8
On the frozen 45-building canonical model, all 630 pair candidates are evaluated with
equivalent outputs by two same-primitive implementations. Three matched repeats yield
median end-to-end times of 11,608.183 s for repeated intersection and 24.037 s for block-
er reuse, including preparation. The resulting ratio is specific to those implementations
and inputs.
    Preserved cohorts show strong same-site aggregate agreement, while controlled re-
plays locate outside-footprint obstructions and remove the observed adverse events in

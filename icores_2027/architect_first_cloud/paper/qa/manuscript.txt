 Efficient Solar-Access Intervention Screening for Early
    Urban Renewal: An Auditable Decision-Support
                        Workflow



        Abstract. Architects considering solar-access improvements in an existing urban
        block need a manageable set of alternatives before investing in detailed design
        and simulation. This paper presents a bounded, auditable screening workflow: 630
        rule-based two-building height-reduction candidates are ranked by declared pref-
        erences and an area–volume Pareto analysis; a curated set of 23 receives archived
        Honeybee/Radiance review; geometric translation and professional judgment re-
        main separate gates. The case contains 45 building groups, of which 36 are
        eligible under research constraints. We report the actual top-ten shortlist with its
        performance, nominal volume costs and review status, rather than treating the
        candidates as completed architectural designs. A separately frozen 20-candidate
        same-site coverage cohort has low-sun-area mean absolute error of 0.45 m2 , re-
        sulting from one 9 m2 grid-cell discrepancy. Historical matched tests support
        fast exhaustive screening: median full-universe runtime falls from 11,608.183
        to 24.037 s through blocker reuse, including precomputation, for two specific
        single-thread implementations. Historical translation replays also expose outside-
        footprint walls; four contained corrections remove the observed adverse sunlight
        events. Current cloud work reanalyses archived aggregates and does not perform
        new site simulations. The contribution is an evidence-linked choice set for further
        architectural assessment, conditional on approximate canonical geometry, a fixed
        sampling protocol and explicit preferences. AI assistance in preparing this abstract
        is disclosed [6].

        Keywords: Decision support · Solar access · Urban renewal · Candidate screening
        · Multi-criteria evaluation


1    Introduction

An architect investigating an existing block rarely begins with a single intervention
whose consequences are already known.1 Several building groups may be eligible for
study, and a modest rule such as reducing the height of two groups immediately creates
hundreds of combinations. Detailed modeling and simulation of every alternative can
consume effort before the useful trade-offs are clear. The early decision is therefore which
candidates deserve closer examination, with what evidence, and under which unresolved
constraints. A useful computational output is a traceable shortlist that preserves the
architect’s ability to question the assumptions and choose a next step.
    Solar access is one part of that decision. Urban environmental analysis distinguishes
irradiation, illuminance and duration of direct exposure [1,2]. We use sampled outdoor
 1 Substantial AI-assisted text preparation in this section [6]; see the AI-Assistance Disclosure.
direct-sun hours and a research threshold to describe low-sun grid area. These quantities
do not measure indoor daylight, annual energy performance or statutory compliance.
Nor do they establish a right to alter the buildings represented by a model. Making these
boundaries visible is part of the decision-support task rather than an incidental caveat.
    This paper asks: under a fixed canonical block model and explicit low-intervention
height rules, can the complete set of 630 building-pair candidates be evaluated efficient-
ly and reduced to an auditable set for further architectural judgment? The workflow
follows a deliberate sequence: define the rule universe; evaluate and rank all candidates;
expose a top-ten list and complementary nondominated alternatives; review a curated
set of 23 through an archived Honeybee/Radiance route; and separately assess translated
geometry before any professional decision. A separately frozen 20-candidate same-site
coverage cohort provides additional, limited agreement evidence. Neither cohort estab-
lishes general accuracy across cities.
    The case contains 45 building groups and a research-defined eligible subset of 36.
Each candidate changes exactly two heights according to one fixed rule and leaves foot-
prints unchanged. Four metrics describe benefits and nominal intervention cost, while
fixed weights express one preference scenario. These are 630 rule-based volumetric
alternatives, not 630 complete designs. Likewise, the top ten are candidates for further
assessment rather than ten construction-ready solutions. Ownership, use, structural safe-
ty, heritage constraints and implementation remain matters for the architect and other
responsible professionals.
    Three contributions serve that decision sequence. First, a fully enumerated and repro-
ducible shortlist connects candidate identities to metrics, score, Pareto membership and
review status. Second, the evidence distinguishes screening agreement, computational
equivalence and design-translation validity, so that a high rank does not silently be-
come a claim of professional approval. Third, historical matched runtime and controlled
translation experiments show how reuse and containment checks support a usable, au-
ditable screening process. The algorithm is application-specific exhaustive screening,
not a trained AI system or a new general optimization method. No architect user study
or realized improvement to a built street is claimed.


2    Related Work and Positioning

Urban solar-performance assessment has long considered the interaction between built
form and the availability of sunlight and daylight.2 Compagnon [1] evaluates solar and
daylight availability in urban fabric through surface-based performance analysis. The
taxonomy of Czachura et al. [2] helps distinguish the metrics used in urban planning. This
distinction matters here: a threshold on sampled direct-sun hours cannot be substituted
for irradiation, daylight autonomy or a jurisdiction-specific compliance test.
    Simulation-based design-support methods already connect changes in building form
to environmental objectives. Konis et al. [5] present an optimization framework for early-
stage passive-design support. De Luca et al. [3] develop a reverse solar-envelope form-
finding method that can accommodate regulatory frameworks. Our study has a narrower
 2 Substantial AI-assisted text preparation in this section [6]; see the AI-Assistance Disclosure.
search space: it exhaustively ranks a fixed set of two-building height-reduction rules,
then treats stepback generation as a separate, fallible translation. We do not benchmark
against those systems or infer that exhaustive enumeration outperforms their broader
form-finding methods.
    The software ecosystem also supplies distinct computational roles. Ladybug supports
parametric environmental analysis [7]; Radiance provides a lighting simulation and
rendering system based on ray tracing [8]; and pvlib supplies solar-energy modeling
implementations including solar-position calculation [4]. These references establish the
context of the tools, not the validity of our particular inputs or recipe. In particular,
the effective solar vectors recovered from the archived Honeybee/Ladybug route are
not numerically identical to the saved pvlib vectors. Cross-route agreement therefore
concerns the actual archived configurations, whereas the efficiency comparison isolates
two implementations using exactly the same geometric primitive and vectors.

didates merit review, whether a translated solid implements its rule, and whether an
intervention is acceptable in practice.4 The first three can be supported by explicit mod-
el records; the last requires professional and stakeholder judgment beyond the present
data. Figure 1 summarizes the progression without implying that every shortlisted can-
didate has a completed design translation.

must remain visible rather than being inferred from a shared pair label.

4.3   Computational Cost and Accounting
Let 𝐵, 𝑃, 𝑇, 𝑀 be the building, receiver, time-sample and candidate counts, and let
𝐺 denote the cost of a ray–footprint intersection. Repeated candidate intersection has
work of order 𝑂 (𝑀 𝐵𝑃𝑇𝐺) before early exits. Reuse requires 𝑂 (𝐵𝑃𝑇𝐺) preparation
and 𝑂 (𝑀 𝑃𝑇) candidate updates and metrics. Polygon complexity and possible indexing
affect 𝐺; it is not a universal constant. Score sorting adds 𝑂 (𝑀 log 𝑀) and the present
direct Pareto scan adds 𝑂 (𝑀 2 ) comparisons.
     Two byte-valued blocker arrays require 2𝐵𝑃𝑇 bytes. At the present dimensions this
is 2,924,640 bytes, approximately 2.79 MiB. This analytic storage count excludes geom-
etry, solar data, integer count arrays, temporaries, indexes and Python overhead. It is not
measured peak process memory. Input preparation, precomputation, candidate process-
ing and output costs must be included consistently when evaluating actual efficiency.
     We therefore compare two specific implementations: A repeats the primitive for each
candidate, retaining first-blocker short-circuiting; B precomputes building blockers and
reuses them. Neither implementation uses a spatial index in this experiment. B’s end-
to-end runtime includes independent precomputation for each repeat. A warm-cache
update-only time would answer a different question and is not used as the denominator
of the reported speedup.

4.4   Containment as a Physical Invariant
Let Ω0 be the baseline opaque solid and Ω𝑐 a translated candidate solid. A genuine
subtraction satisfies Ω𝑐 ⊆ Ω0 . For fixed receiving points and solar rays, a ray unob-
structed by Ω0 cannot become obstructed by a subset of Ω0 . Thus containment implies
nondecreasing sampled direct-sun exposure. An adverse event under these assumptions
requires examination of geometry, ray mapping, receiving-point mapping or computa-
tion; it cannot simply be justified as an unavoidable trade-off of subtraction.
    Historical stepback generation is therefore audited independently of score ranking.
The audit checks containment of generated solids, preservation of expected faces, actual
ray intersections for adverse visibility events, and whole-scene union volumes. The
corrected generator constrains the translated geometry to the original solid footprint and
is evaluated in separate runs. Containment is not volume matching: clipping an outside
region may change the amount removed, and overlapping building footprints make per-
building sums differ from scene-union differences. We preserve both the original and
corrected outputs rather than replacing the history with the favorable result.


5     Validation and Experimental Setup

5.1   Evidence Layers and Frozen Inputs

Three historical evidence layers and the current aggregate reanalysis are kept separate.5
The matched implementation experiment uses the canonical real-case input and the com-
plete fixed candidate order. The same-metric agreement study recomputes statistics from
frozen archived aggregates for a curated cohort of 23 and a separately preserved cover-
age cohort of 20. Finally, controlled Honeybee/Radiance replays revisit a baseline and
five historical translations, followed by four corrected scenes. The original 20-candidate
sample was neither reselected nor rerun in the correction experiment. The present cloud
analysis reads archived candidate and validation tables, recomputes selection outputs
and checks their consistency. Original site geometry, ordered coordinates and complete
solver scenes are not available to this cloud analysis, so it performs no new site Honey-
bee/Radiance simulation. The historical timing and scene-replay measurements reported
below retain their original execution context.
    The coverage cohort was selected for score-stratum, volume and identifier coverage
and held out from curated review. It is an outcome-blind same-site coverage sample,
not a probability sample or an independent site. Archived timestamps report freezing
before its Honeybee execution; a file hash verifies unchanged bytes but does not by itself
prove that chronology. We use descriptive agreement measures without a population-
generalization claim.
    A pre-benchmark amendment repaired a normalization-source hash mismatch, pin-
ning the actual committed bytes and an independent frozen reference. All 630 candidate
identities and metrics agreed within existing tolerance. The original failed check was
retained; score bounds were not adapted to outcomes.

5.3   Cross-Route Agreement and Temporal Audit

Within each preserved cohort, the screening route is compared to the archived Honeybee
route using the same metric on both sides: 𝐴 with 𝐴, 𝐻 with 𝐻 and 𝑆 with 𝑆. Scores use
unchanged full-universe bounds. We report Spearman correlation with average ranks for
ties and candidate-level mean absolute error (MAE). Shared 𝑉 inputs and the constant
𝑁 term restrict the independence of the composite-score comparison.
    Exact-𝐾 sets use descending metric followed by natural numeric pair order. The
legacy source-row-order tie rule and inclusion of all boundary ties are reported as
sensitivities. Every such result belongs to its comparison cohort; no selected-cohort
statistic is interpreted as recall across all 630 candidates. Numerical zero p-values are
not interpreted as exact probability zero, and significance claims are unnecessary for
these nonprobability cohorts.
    Actual archived WEA records, hour-of-year labels, solar-source names and all 16
visibility columns were reconciled across the six historical scenes. Every cumulative
exposure equals its binary visibility sum multiplied by 0.5 h. The WEA inputs have
constant direct-normal irradiance of 1000 W/m2 and diffuse-horizontal irradiance of
zero. They carry the direct-sun-hours analysis rather than measured weather. WEA/hour-
of-year records lack a calendar-year field, so the archived Ladybug reconstruction is
not an identical year-specific astronomical input to the declared 2026 pvlib sequence.
Effective rays are shared across the controlled historical scenes but differ measurably
from the saved pvlib rays. North zero remains an assumption.

6     Results

6.1   The Actual Rule-Candidate Shortlist

The central output is the score-ordered choice set in Table 2.6 The table keeps the original
production preference weights and full-universe normalization bounds; it is not retuned
to favor particular pairs. Its performance columns describe uniform-height rules on the
fixed canonical model. They must not be replaced with similarly named translated-
design outcomes. The complete candidate and selection records support inspection of
alternatives outside the displayed ten.
     C9/C24 ranks first with 𝐴 = 396 m2 , 𝐻 = 0.04874 h and nominal 𝑉 = 5551.333 m3 .
Its rule reduces the modeled heights of C9 and C24 from 12 and 9 m to 9 and 6.75 m.
C9/C15 offers 351 m2 with a smaller nominal proxy of 3127.755 m3 , while C9/C13
offers 333 m2 at 2975.156 m3 . These three are also on the full-universe area–volume

333 m2 and 0.03028 h. C12/C24 ranks seventh despite a smaller area benefit of 198 m2 ,

6.3   Measured Reuse Efficiency

All 12 measured scale/repeat comparisons pass pointwise and aggregate equivalence
checks. Table 4 reports the scale-level medians. At 630 candidates, repeated intersection
takes 11,608.183 s and reuse takes 24.037 s including precomputation; their ratio is
482.927. The relative benefit increases with the candidate count as repeated geometry
work accumulates while preparation is amortized.
    This is an implementation comparison, not a general solver comparison. In partic-
ular, the repeated-intersection implementation is not a spatial-index baseline, and the
experiment does not time equivalent Honeybee scenes against B. Historical screening
and Honeybee timings with different machines, solver semantics or worker counts are
excluded from the ratio. The result supports the benefit of reuse in the measured setting,
while leaving the benefit relative to other optimized implementations unmeasured.

6.4   Translation Failure and Controlled Correction
Four of five historical translations fail the solid-containment check. In C9/C24 and
C13/C24, rays that were clear in the baseline intersect upper walls of C24 outside its
original footprint. Each of these scenes has four worsened receiving points and 9 m2 of
newly low-sun grid area in the retained historical result. The actual ray hits identify added
obstructions as the cause. Synthetic tests had also exposed hole-handling and silent-face
risks, but the canonical site has no holes and the six archived face dictionaries regenerate
exactly; those synthetic defects are not asserted as the cause of this case.
    The four contained corrections have no adverse visibility events, worsened receiving
points or newly low-sun grid area on the fixed model, rays and grid. Figure 3 retains
the before/after distinction. Low-sun-area reduction changes from 342 to 351 m2 for
C9/C24 and from 207 to 216 m2 for C13/C24. C9/C15 and C6/C33 remain at 342 and
18 m2 . The already-contained C9/C13 remains at 333 m2 through reuse of its unchanged
historical result, not an additional correction run.

7     Discussion and Limitations

7.1   Using the Shortlist in Architectural Work

The immediate value of the workflow is a smaller, documented choice set.7 An architect
can compare candidate identities and benefits without manually creating 630 detailed
models, while retaining the option to inspect the whole universe. The score provides one
declared ordering; the Pareto set keeps lower-volume alternatives visible. The review
status identifies which numbers have a second computational route and which translated
forms still require assessment. This is a proposed use of the outputs, not a measured
reduction in architects’ working time or evidence from a user study.
    The next practical decision is not necessarily to select rank one. The architect may
reject a highly ranked pair because the modeled eligibility flag does not reflect ownership,
function, protected fabric or structural constraints. A lower-ranked candidate may merit
further work for reasons not represented in 𝐴/𝐻/𝑉/𝑁. Those judgments should be
recorded alongside, rather than hidden inside, an unexplained change to the score. If the
team changes the modeled eligibility, geometry, rule, threshold or preference scenario,
the result becomes a new explicitly versioned analysis; the frozen results should remain
available for comparison.
    Translation is a further decision gate. The historical C9/C24 result illustrates why
adverse sunlight cannot be accepted uncritically as the natural price of a subtractive
intervention. Actual rays hit walls that had been generated outside the baseline footprint.
Containment and re-evaluation corrected that error, while volume accounting showed
that clipping was not volume matching. Consequently, the updated evidence does not
justify a permanent rule that only previously favored pairs may proceed. It supplies
corrected outcomes for comparison and requires the architect to examine the intended
intervention, verified boundaries and relevant costs.
    Fast evaluation makes this bounded review process computationally practical under
the measured conditions, but it does not solve omitted decisions. The 24.037 s historical
implementation time concerns the canonical 630-rule calculation and includes block-
er preparation. It excludes an architect’s model preparation, stakeholder consultation,
structural investigation and detailed design. The broader human workflow was not timed.
The contribution is therefore an auditable computational component for early selection,
with explicit handoff information and limits.


7.2   Threats to Validity

Canonical geometry. The DXF component-bounding-box unions, shared components,
overlapping footprints and OBJ disagreements are the most important external-validity
limits. Reproducing a canonical model across implementations cannot validate its as-
built fidelity. Correcting those boundaries would define a new input version and require
an explicit assessment of affected results. Current cohort agreement must not be trans-
ferred to that new geometry without evidence.
 7 Substantial AI-assisted text preparation in this section [6]; see the AI-Assistance Disclosure.
Restricted physical scope. A single day, 16 samples, a 3 m grid, a 0.1 m receiving
height and an assumed north orientation delimit the reported performance. Threshold
counts can change discontinuously near 3 h. The constant WEA carrier is not measured
meteorology, and the study does not validate indoor illuminance, glare, annual weather,
energy production or regulatory compliance. The distinction between saved pvlib rays
and archived Ladybug rays remains part of the cross-route comparison rather than being
suppressed to imply identical astronomical inputs.

Comparison design. The matched timing comparison controls the geometric primitive
and single-thread execution, but covers only one canonical model and two implementa-
tions without spatial indexing. Three repeats quantify the recorded execution conditions;
they do not characterize hardware populations or arbitrary polygon complexity. We do
not claim a measured memory reduction from the analytic blocker-array size or unavail-
able RSS values. A stronger efficiency comparison would include indexed and compiled
baselines with the same accounting contract.

Cohorts and metrics. Curated review and outcome-blind coverage serve different pur-
poses and remain separate. Neither is a random multi-site sample. Shared volume inputs,
constant 𝑁, discretized area, ties and preference weights constrain the interpretation of
rank agreement. Candidate-level aggregate errors can mask spatial differences. The
frozen 20 were not selectively rerun after the translation audit because no shared re-
ceiver, temporal-parser or missing-face defect was found in the actual scenes. A newly
demonstrated shared defect would require an affected-scope review rather than selective
retention of favorable outputs.

Correction scope. Four corrected translations remove the observed adverse events under
fixed rays and receivers. This supports the tested containment correction on this canonical
model, not a guarantee for arbitrary imported meshes. Containment does not ensure
equal volume, structural feasibility or a preferred design. The nominal 𝑉 proxy should
be replaced or supplemented by an appropriate audited quantity if the decision requires
physical removal costs.

7.3   Reproducibility and Responsible Release
The evidence separates historically measured runtime and scene replays, current frozen-
table recomputation and reused historical results. Run identifiers, source, configuration
and input hashes, raw timing repeats, aggregate tables, equivalence checks, correction
ledgers and figure-source manifests are retained. The final integrated test record contains
112 passing tests; this supports software regression coverage, not physical ground truth.
Failures and amendments remain part of the record rather than being overwritten.
    Raw CAD, DXF, SKP and OBJ inputs, receiver coordinates, WEA carriers, Honey-
bee models and Radiance intermediate data remain restricted. Figures in this manuscript
show aggregate evidence without site geometry or receiver coordinates. No public arti-
fact release or unrestricted replication right is asserted. Any external evidence package
must be reviewed for rights and anonymity before distribution. This restriction limit-
s independent reproduction of the site-specific experiment and should remain visible
alongside the available aggregate and algorithmic detail.
8    Conclusion

This study returns a bounded solar-access search to the architectural decision it supports:
which interventions deserve further examination?8 Under fixed research eligibility and
height rules, all 630 building-pair candidates are evaluated, a score-based top ten and
area–volume alternatives are exposed, and a curated 23-candidate review pool links
screening to archived Honeybee/Radiance evidence. The resulting choice set preserves
the distinction between a ranked rule, a reviewed rule, a checked translation and a
professionally acceptable intervention.
    Historical supporting experiments make that workflow more auditable. Two matched
implementations preserve pointwise and aggregate results while reducing the full-
universe median runtime from 11,608.183 to 24.037 s, including reuse preparation.
Preserved same-site cohorts show strong aggregate agreement within their sampling
and metric limits. Controlled translation replays identify erroneous outside-footprint
obstructions, and four contained corrections remove the observed adverse events on the
fixed rays and grid. The current cloud reanalysis reorganizes and recomputes archived
evidence; it adds no new site simulation.
    The workflow does not establish as-built accuracy, statutory compliance, volume-
matched construction quantities or an automatically approved design. Its next substantive
extensions are verified boundaries, broader site and preference coverage, additional op-
timized computational baselines and direct evaluation of how architects use the shortlist.
For the present case, its defensible output is a transparent range of candidates and review
evidence that an architect can scrutinize before deciding what to develop.

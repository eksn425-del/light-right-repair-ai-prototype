Auditable Solar-Access Decision Support through Exact
    Blocker Reuse and Containment-Constrained
                     Translation



       Abstract. Early-stage solar-access decisions require efficient comparison of in-
       terventions and reliable translation of simplified rules into geometry. We study
       a bounded decision-support workflow that selects two of 36 eligible building
       groups in a 45-building canonical model, giving 630 uniform-height-reduction
       candidates. Building-level occlusion reuse evaluates every candidate with the
       same ray–footprint primitive as repeated intersection, while a separate contain-
       ment audit checks translated stepback solids. Across three matched single-thread
       repeats, full-universe median runtimes are 11,608.183 s for repeated intersection
       and 24.037 s for reuse, including precomputation, with equivalent pointwise ex-
       posure, metrics, scores, ranks and Pareto results. This 482.927-fold ratio applies
       to these two implementations, not to arbitrary solar-analysis software. Frozen
       same-site comparison cohorts of 23 and 20 candidates show strong aggregate
       agreement with archived Honeybee/Radiance results; the 20-candidate low-sun-
       area mean absolute error is 0.45 m2 , arising from one candidate-level difference of
       9 m2 . Six controlled historical replays identify outside-footprint walls as the cause
       of adverse sunlight changes. Four separately simulated contained corrections re-
       move those adverse events on the fixed grid and rays. The canonical geometry
       remains a component-bounding-box approximation with overlapping footprints.
       The contribution is an auditable computational screening and translation work-
       flow, conditional on that model, rather than as-built validation or an automatically
       approved design. Drafting and editing assistance is disclosed for this abstract and
       the paper [8].

       Keywords: Decision support · Solar access · Exhaustive screening · Occlusion
       reuse · Geometry containment


1   Introduction

Early-stage changes to urban form pose a decision-support problem: planners need to
compare many interventions, but a favorable simulation score is useful only if the eval-
uated geometry implements the intended change. [8] Solar-access screening therefore
requires both an efficient evaluation procedure and an explicit check on the feasibility of
translating a rule into a solid model. Here solar access means sampled outdoor direct-sun
duration, distinct from irradiation, indoor daylight and annual energy performance [1,2].
    Two stages of a common workflow expose different risks. During screening, candi-
dates that change only a small part of a scene repeatedly evaluate unchanged obstructions.
During design translation, a nominally subtractive stepback may inadvertently place ma-
terial outside the original building footprint. Faster ranking addresses the first problem
but cannot resolve the second: an invalid translation can invalidate the premise of a
favorable candidate without changing the correctness of its original score.
    We study a deliberately bounded case: all 630 pairs from 36 eligible building groups
in a 45-building canonical model. A fixed height-reduction rule defines each candidate;
four reported metrics, a declared preference score and an area–volume Pareto analysis
support comparison. The decision universe is fixed rather than learned or expanded
adaptively. Eligibility is a research constraint, not permission to alter a property, and the
canonical geometry is an approximate model rather than an as-built survey.
    The paper makes three application-specific contributions:

 1. An exact blocker-count update for the declared fixed-footprint rule universe, with
    explicit conditions for equivalence to repeated geometric intersection. Exactness
    concerns reuse of a common primitive, not physical ground truth or equivalence to
    another ray tracer.
 2. A matched end-to-end implementation comparison that includes precomputation
    and checks pointwise exposure, metrics, scores, ranks and Pareto membership before
    reporting runtime. Frozen same-site Honeybee/Radiance comparisons provide a
    separate, qualified assessment of aggregate agreement.
 3. A containment-based check on design translation, supported by controlled replays
    that locate historical adverse ray events and evaluate separately generated contained
    corrections. Failed historical outcomes remain visible, and geometric containment
    is distinguished from volume matching and professional feasibility.

Together these contributions connect computational screening to a verifiable translation
step. The objective is not a new general optimization algorithm, but evidence about
when a reusable evaluation and a subtraction-based geometric interpretation are trust-
worthy within a declared model. The results address efficiency, cross-route agreement
and translation validity separately; none establishes unrestricted solver superiority or
automatic design approval.


2   Related Work and Positioning

Urban solar assessment relates built form to several distinct performance objectives. [8]
Compagnon [1] examines solar and daylight availability in urban fabric, while Czachura
et al. [2] organize solar-performance metrics for planning. These studies motivate an
explicit choice of decision metric: the thresholded direct-sun-duration measure used here
is not interchangeable with irradiation, daylight autonomy or a statutory compliance test.
    Simulation-based building optimization provides a broader setting for repeated per-
formance evaluation. Nguyen et al. [7] review its methods, applications and compu-
tational challenges. Konis et al. [5] connect building form and passive-performance
objectives, and De Luca et al. [3] develop reverse solar-envelope form finding with
regulatory constraints. Our search is narrower: a finite set of prescribed two-building
reductions is exhaustively evaluated, then translated geometries are checked separately.
We do not compare search efficiency or design quality against these broader form-finding
approaches.
    Computational reuse should also be distinguished from multifidelity modeling. Pe-
herstorfer et al. [9] survey methods that combine models of different cost and fidelity.
In the present timing comparison, A and B use the same geometric primitive and solar
vectors; B reorganizes identical blocker contributions rather than replacing the primi-
tive with a lower-fidelity surrogate. The archived Honeybee comparison is a separate
cross-route check, not a calibrated multifidelity estimator, and its effective solar vectors
differ from those used by the screening route.
    Performance ranking alone does not specify a satisfactory design decision. Mueller
and Ochsendorf [6] combine designer preferences with structural performance in con-
ceptual design. Here the fixed score makes one declared preference ordering repro-
ducible, while the area–volume Pareto view exposes a limited trade-off. Neither resolves
ownership, construction, structural or regulatory constraints. Our containment test ad-
dresses a more basic prerequisite: whether the translated solid satisfies the subtraction
premise under which sunlight cannot worsen on fixed rays.
    Ladybug [10], Radiance [11] and pvlib [4] supply environmental-analysis, ray-
tracing and solar-modeling context. Their established roles do not validate this study’s
particular geometry or protocol. The contribution lies in keeping reuse equivalence,
aggregate agreement, preference ranking and geometric feasibility distinct while con-
necting them in one auditable decision-support case.

4.2   Computational Cost and Accounting
Let 𝐵, 𝑃, 𝑇, 𝑀 be the building, receiver, time-sample and candidate counts, and let
𝐺 denote the cost of a ray–footprint intersection. Repeated candidate intersection has
work of order 𝑂 (𝑀 𝐵𝑃𝑇𝐺) before early exits. Reuse requires 𝑂 (𝐵𝑃𝑇𝐺) preparation
and 𝑂 (𝑀 𝑃𝑇) candidate updates and metrics. Polygon complexity and possible indexing
affect 𝐺; it is not a universal constant. Score sorting adds 𝑂 (𝑀 log 𝑀) and the present
direct Pareto scan adds 𝑂 (𝑀 2 ) comparisons.
     Two byte-valued blocker arrays require 2𝐵𝑃𝑇 bytes. At the present dimensions this
is 2,924,640 bytes, approximately 2.79 MiB. This analytic storage count excludes geom-
etry, solar data, integer count arrays, temporaries, indexes and Python overhead. It is not
measured peak process memory. Input preparation, precomputation, candidate process-
ing and output costs must be included consistently when evaluating actual efficiency.
     We therefore compare two specific implementations: A repeats the primitive for each
candidate, retaining first-blocker short-circuiting; B precomputes building blockers and
reuses them. Neither implementation uses a spatial index in this experiment. B’s end-
to-end runtime includes independent precomputation for each repeat. A warm-cache
update-only time would answer a different question and is not used as the denominator
of the reported speedup.

4.3   Containment as a Physical Invariant
Let Ω0 be the baseline opaque solid and Ω𝑐 a translated candidate solid. A genuine
subtraction satisfies Ω𝑐 ⊆ Ω0 . For fixed receiving points and solar rays, a ray unob-
structed by Ω0 cannot become obstructed by a subset of Ω0 . Thus containment implies
nondecreasing sampled direct-sun exposure. An adverse event under these assumptions
requires examination of geometry, ray mapping, receiving-point mapping or computa-
tion; it cannot simply be justified as an unavoidable trade-off of subtraction.
    Historical stepback generation is therefore audited independently of score ranking.
The audit checks containment of generated solids, preservation of expected faces, actual
ray intersections for adverse visibility events, and whole-scene union volumes. The
corrected generator constrains the translated geometry to the original solid footprint and
is evaluated in separate runs. Containment is not volume matching: clipping an outside
region may change the amount removed, and overlapping building footprints make per-
building sums differ from scene-union differences. We preserve both the original and
corrected outputs rather than replacing the history with the favorable result.


5     Experimental Protocol

5.1   Evidence Layers and Frozen Inputs

Three evidence layers are kept separate. [8] The matched implementation experiment
uses the canonical real-case input and the complete fixed candidate order. The same-
metric agreement study recomputes statistics from frozen archived aggregates for a
curated cohort of 23 and a separately preserved coverage cohort of 20. Finally, controlled
Honeybee/Radiance replays revisit a baseline and five historical translations, followed
by four corrected scenes. The original 20-candidate sample is neither reselected nor
rerun in the correction experiment. The timing measurements and scene replays were
executed in the original author-side Windows environment. Manuscript preparation and
revision reprocess archived evidence, regenerate plots and compile the paper; they do
not constitute new physical-simulation runs.
    The coverage cohort was selected for score-stratum, volume and identifier coverage
and held out from curated review. It is an outcome-blind same-site coverage sample,
not a probability sample or an independent site. Archived timestamps report freezing
before its Honeybee execution; a file hash verifies unchanged bytes but does not by itself
prove that chronology. We use descriptive agreement measures without a population-
generalization claim.
    Before benchmarking, a normalization-source identity mismatch was reconciled
against an independent frozen reference. All 630 candidate identities and metrics agreed
within existing tolerance; the amended provenance and original failure remain in the
evidence record. Score bounds were not adapted to outcomes.


5.2   Matched Runtime and Equivalence

The runtime experiment uses nested fixed prefixes of 5, 20, 60 and 630 candidates,
with three measured A/B repeats at each size. Warm-up and pilot runs are excluded
from the reported medians. Each comparison fixes geometry, ordered receivers, solar
vectors, tolerance, candidate order, score bounds and phase accounting. Equivalence
is checked for pointwise duration, 𝐴/𝐻/𝑉/𝑁, scores, ranks and Pareto membership
before reporting runtime ratios. The 630-candidate A runs were completed; their times
are measured rather than extrapolated.
    The environment is Windows 11, Python 3.12.2, NumPy 2.4.6, Shapely 2.1.2 with
GEOS 3.13.1, and pvlib 0.15.2. The processor is recorded as AMD64 Family 25 Model
80 Stepping 0, with 16 logical CPUs available. The experiment uses one process and
one thread; common numerical-library thread environment variables are fixed to one.
Elapsed time is measured with time.perf_counter. The record does not supply a veri-
fied commercial CPU model name, and unavailable Windows resident-set measurements
are left unknown rather than reported as zero.

5.3   Cross-Route Agreement and Temporal Audit
Within each preserved cohort, the screening route is compared to the archived Honeybee
route using the same metric on both sides: 𝐴 with 𝐴, 𝐻 with 𝐻 and 𝑆 with 𝑆. Scores use
unchanged full-universe bounds. We report Spearman correlation with average ranks for
ties and candidate-level mean absolute error (MAE). Shared 𝑉 inputs and the constant
𝑁 term restrict the independence of the composite-score comparison.
    Exact-𝐾 sets use descending metric followed by natural numeric pair order. The
legacy source-row-order tie rule and inclusion of all boundary ties are reported as
sensitivities. Every such result belongs to its comparison cohort; no selected-cohort
statistic is interpreted as recall across all 630 candidates. Numerical zero p-values are
not interpreted as exact probability zero, and significance claims are unnecessary for
these nonprobability cohorts.
    Actual archived WEA records, hour-of-year labels, solar-source names and all 16
visibility columns were reconciled across the six historical scenes. Every cumulative
exposure equals its binary visibility sum multiplied by 0.5 h. The WEA inputs have
constant direct-normal irradiance of 1000 W/m2 and diffuse-horizontal irradiance of
zero. They carry the direct-sun-hours analysis rather than measured weather. WEA/hour-
of-year records lack a calendar-year field, so the archived Ladybug reconstruction is
not an identical year-specific astronomical input to the declared 2026 pvlib sequence.
Effective rays are shared across the controlled historical scenes but differ measurably
from the saved pvlib rays. North zero remains an assumption.

5.4   Controlled Translation Replays
Six controlled replays comprise the baseline and five historical translated pairs: C9/C24,
C9/C15, C9/C13, C13/C24 and C6/C33. Their ordered receiver grid, solar sources, time
columns and archived scene identity are checked. New runs use one CPU and one
worker. All six reproduce their historical pointwise visibility matrices exactly. Four
contained corrections are then simulated separately. C9/C13 was already contained and
is not simulated again as a correction; its unchanged value is reused only for comparison.
There are therefore ten new scene runs, not eleven, and no full 630-scene Honeybee
experiment.

6.3   Translation Failure and Controlled Correction

Four of five historical translations fail the solid-containment check. In C9/C24 and
C13/C24, rays that were clear in the baseline intersect upper walls of C24 outside its
original footprint. Each of these scenes has four worsened receiving points and 9 m2 of
newly low-sun grid area in the retained historical result. The actual ray hits identify added
obstructions as the cause. Synthetic tests had also exposed hole-handling and silent-face
risks, but the canonical site has no holes and the six archived face dictionaries regenerate
exactly; those synthetic defects are not asserted as the cause of this case.
    The four contained corrections have no adverse visibility events, worsened receiving
points or newly low-sun grid area on the fixed model, rays and grid. Figure 3 retains
the before/after distinction. Low-sun-area reduction changes from 342 to 351 m2 for
C9/C24 and from 207 to 216 m2 for C13/C24. C9/C15 and C6/C33 remain at 342 and
18 m2 . The already-contained C9/C13 remains at 333 m2 through reuse of its unchanged
historical result, not an additional correction run.
    For any translated solid, the signed net union-volume reduction is |Ω0 | − |Ω𝑐 |. For
a noncontained legacy solid, this equals removed baseline volume minus added outside
volume; it is not gross removed material. Table 4 shows why containment correction
must not be called a volume-matched comparison. Nominal rule budgets, rule-solid net

7     Discussion and Limitations

7.1   Implications for Decision Support

The workflow separates three decisions that should not be collapsed into a single s-
core. [8] First, exhaustive screening identifies preferred candidates within the declared
rule universe. Reuse makes that comparison less expensive without introducing a new
approximation relative to the common primitive. Second, the preference score and two-
objective Pareto view organize alternatives under explicit criteria; they do not determine
which criteria stakeholders should value. Third, the translation check tests whether
the constructed geometry preserves the intended subtraction before its performance is
interpreted.
    This separation changes how a favorable result should be used. A highly ranked
rule candidate warrants further examination, not automatic acceptance of a generated
solid. The historical added-wall failure demonstrates why downstream feasibility must
be checked independently of ranking. Containment is a necessary consistency check
for a pure-subtraction interpretation; it is not a complete feasibility certificate. Actual
removal cost, structural safety, legal rights and regulatory acceptability still require
appropriate quantities, verified boundaries and professional review.
    The practical value is therefore a reproducible shortlist with explicit reasons to
accept, reject or revisit a translation. Retaining failed and corrected outcomes makes
the decision traceable and prevents a geometric repair from being misrepresented as
an improvement in the original ranking model. Extensions to another geometry, height
rule or solar protocol require new blocker contributions and renewed checks; the present
implementation comparison cannot establish their performance in advance.


7.2   Threats to Validity

Canonical geometry. DXF component-bounding-box unions, shared components, over-
lapping footprints and OBJ disagreements limit external validity. Reproducing this
model across implementations does not establish as-built fidelity. Revised boundaries
would define a new input version requiring renewed evaluation; current cohort agreement
cannot be transferred without evidence.

Restricted physical scope. A single day, 16 samples, a 3 m grid, a 0.1 m receiving height
and assumed north delimit the results. Threshold counts may change discontinuously
near 3 h. Constant WEA inputs are not measured meteorology; indoor daylight, glare,
annual energy and regulatory compliance are outside scope. Different pvlib and Ladybug
rays preclude an identical-astronomical-input interpretation of cross-route agreement.

Comparison design. The timing experiment controls the primitive and single-thread
execution but covers one model and two implementations without spatial indexing.
Three repeats do not characterize hardware populations or arbitrary polygons. Analytic
blocker storage is not measured peak memory. Indexed and compiled baselines with
identical accounting remain necessary for broader efficiency claims.
Cohorts and metrics. Curated review and outcome-blind coverage serve different pur-
poses and remain separate. Neither is a random multi-site sample. Shared volume inputs,
constant 𝑁, discretized area, ties and preference weights constrain the interpretation of
rank agreement. Candidate-level aggregate errors can mask spatial differences. The
frozen 20 were not selectively rerun after the translation audit because no shared re-
ceiver, temporal-parser or missing-face defect was found in the actual scenes. A newly
demonstrated shared defect would require an affected-scope review rather than selective
retention of favorable outputs.

Correction scope. The four corrections support containment on the tested model and
rays, not arbitrary imported meshes. Containment neither matches volumes nor estab-
lishes structural feasibility or preference. Decisions about physical removal costs require
an audited quantity beyond the nominal 𝑉 proxy.


7.3   Reproducibility and Responsible Release

The evidence separates measured runtime, newly executed scene replays, frozen-table
statistical recomputation and historical reused results. Run identifiers, source, config-
uration and input hashes, raw timing repeats, aggregate tables, equivalence checks,
correction ledgers and figure-source manifests are retained. The recorded 112 passing
integrated tests provide software regression evidence, not physical ground truth; failures
and amendments are retained alongside them.
    Raw CAD, DXF, SKP and OBJ inputs, receiver coordinates, WEA carriers, Honey-
bee models and Radiance intermediate data remain restricted. Figures in this manuscript
show aggregate evidence without site geometry or receiver coordinates. No public arti-
fact release or unrestricted replication right is asserted. Any external evidence package
must be reviewed for rights and anonymity before distribution. This restriction limit-
s independent reproduction of the site-specific experiment and should remain visible
alongside the available aggregate and algorithmic detail.


8     Conclusion

A bounded solar-access screening problem can reuse exact building-level occlusion
contributions while keeping its preference model and geometric limitations explicit. [8]
On the frozen 45-building canonical model, all 630 pair candidates are evaluated with
equivalent outputs by two same-primitive implementations. Three matched repeats yield
median end-to-end times of 11,608.183 s for repeated intersection and 24.037 s for block-
er reuse, including preparation. The resulting ratio is specific to those implementations
and inputs.
    Preserved cohorts show strong same-site aggregate agreement, while controlled re-
plays locate outside-footprint obstructions and remove the observed adverse events in
four contained corrections. The results support auditable screening, not as-built accu-
racy, volume-matched interventions or automatic design approval. Verified boundaries,
broader site coverage and additional optimized baselines are the next substantive exten-
sions.

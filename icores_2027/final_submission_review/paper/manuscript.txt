       Solar-Access Intervention Screening and
      Preference-Sensitivity Decision Support for
                   Urban Renewal



        Abstract. Insufficient sunlight in existing urban blocks calls for inter-
        vention screening that makes both environmental benefits and decision
        assumptions visible. We integrate three archived experimental rounds for
        630 two-building height-reduction candidates in one simplified site: fixed-
        score and Pareto screening, preference-regret analysis, and a prospec-
        tively frozen solar-priority comparison. Area benefit, mean direct-sun
        gain and nominal removed volume discriminate candidates; newly low-
        sun area is zero throughout the rule universe. The historical 45/35/15/5
        weights are researcher-declared, not expert-validated. A ten-candidate
        regret heuristic improves full-simplex mean utility regret from 0.010759
        to 0.000054, but the historical list already has zero regret under ordered
        and local priorities. In the third round, none of six contexts passes the
        complete preregistered replacement rule. Total-sunlight-weight scenar-
        ios improve winner coverage, including 89.08% to 100% under Solar70,
        but recovery gains stay below 0.02 and some lists fail sunlight-benefit
        guards. New alternatives also expose missing Honeybee review. Selec-
        tive simulation and containment checks remain separate evidence lay-
        ers. The contribution is an application decision model, an interpretable
        preference-sensitive workflow and traceable layered validation, not a new
        general algorithm or a construction-ready recommendation. AI assistance
        is disclosed [9].

        Keywords: Decision support · Preference sensitivity · Solar access ·
        Multicriteria analysis · Urban renewal


1     Introduction

Urban renewal can seek to relieve insufficient sunlight while limiting intervention
in existing buildings.1 Before detailed design, an architect needs a manageable
set of alternatives and a clear account of why they were retained. Ranking mod-
eled sunlight gains alone neglects intervention burden; treating a weighted score
as an authoritative answer hides whose priorities it represents. The task here is
early-stage decision support: identify promising height reductions, expose pref-
erence dependence and direct later review, while leaving design selection to the
architect.
    We examine one simplified urban block with 45 canonical building groups,
36 research-eligible groups and all 630 unordered two-building pairs under a

fixed height-reduction rule. Four metrics express the decision rationale: low-sun-
area reduction (A) captures threshold relief, mean direct-sun gain (H) captures
exposure improvements beyond threshold crossings, nominal removal volume
(V ) approximates intervention magnitude, and newly low-sun area (N ) flags
adverse effects. The historical score weights these terms 45%, 35%, 15% and
5%. These are fixed researcher choices, neither elicited expert weights nor an
analytic-hierarchy-process result. Since N = 0 throughout this rule universe, its
contribution is constant.
    Three existing rounds are integrated without new experiments. The first
establishes finite screening, historical Top10/Pareto alternatives, selective sim-
ulation review and geometry checks. The second tests rank acceptability and
regret across broad, ordered and local weight spaces. The third asks whether a
solar-priority shortlist retains more preference-specific top-ten alternatives while
meeting prospectively frozen safeguards. The rounds answer different questions:
preserving one best opportunity is not the same as retaining many highly ranked
options, and broad preference uncertainty is not synonymous with a sunlight-first
project brief.
    Our contributions are (1) an explicit finite solar-intervention decision mod-
el with interpretable A/H/V /N semantics; (2) a preference-sensitive screening
workflow that compares equal review budgets and retains negative results; and
(3) a traceable validation structure distinguishing rule evaluation, selective pro-
fessional simulation and translated geometry. The mathematical ingredients are
established. No new general algorithm, validated professional preference model
or observed improvement in architects’ decision quality is claimed.
    The evidence supports conditional opportunity preservation rather than un-
conditional method replacement. Broad-space regret improves, yet the historical
list already has zero regret in the ordered and local spaces. The third round
fails its full replacement gate in all six contexts, despite useful winner-coverage
improvements in total-sunlight spaces. All preference analyses reuse archived
performances; they add no Honeybee/Radiance runs, site observations or archi-
tect surveys.


2     Related Work and Methodological Position

2.1     Uncertain Preferences and Representative Subsets

SMAA-2 extends stochastic multicriteria acceptability analysis to rank positions,
allowing the analyst to examine alternatives over weight space rather than se-
lecting one weight vector [6].2 Monte Carlo approximation of these acceptability
integrals is an established implementation approach [11]. We use this restricted
idea with fixed criterion values and explicitly declared weight distributions. We
do not implement uncertain performance distributions, central weight vectors or
confidence factors, and do not equate acceptability with the probability that a
real architect likes a candidate.
2
    AI-assisted preparation [9]; scope is stated in the AI Use Disclosure.
    Robust ordinal regression instead retains additive value functions compatible
with decision-maker comparisons, distinguishing necessary from possible prefer-
ences [4]. No such comparisons are available in this study. Consequently, our
weight restrictions are research scenarios, not a fitted ordinal-regression model.
The distinction also prevents treating unanimous sampled rankings as necessary
preferences over a continuous domain. A future elicitation stage could constrain
the admissible model set using actual architect judgments; it is not part of the
present evidence.
    Minimax regret compares the utility of a decision against the best feasible
alternative under compatible utility functions. Boutilier et al. [1] develop op-
timization and elicitation methods for this criterion. Our output is a subset
from which a later decision can be made, rather than one irreversible choice.
Regret-minimizing representative databases already formalize bounded subsets
whose best attainable utility approximates that of the full database [7]. Their
relative-regret setting and algorithmic guarantees should not be transferred to
our absolute normalized loss or greedy implementation. Neither regret-based
subset selection nor exploration of weight space is claimed as an invention here.


2.2     Architectural and Environmental Decision Support

Building-performance optimization already covers multiobjective design, com-
putational efficiency, uncertainty and adoption challenges [8]. EvoMass connects
optimization-based building-massing exploration with typological interpretation
and designer reconfiguration [12]. Its scope is broader than the fixed interven-
tion rules considered here. It provides relevant architectural context, but its
user-study evidence does not validate this workflow or our preferences. We do
not claim the first architect-in-the-loop optimization process.
    Solar-performance assessment also uses different physical quantities. Urban
solar and daylight availability [2] and the taxonomy of solar planning metrics [3]
motivate careful naming of the outcome. Our direct-sun duration and threshold
area are not indoor illuminance, annual irradiation or regulatory compliance. La-
dybug [10], Radiance [13] and pvlib [5] provide tool context, rather than guaran-
tees about this case’s input geometry or simulation recipe. The decision-support
analysis sits between fast finite enumeration and selective professional review: it
determines which modeled alternatives should remain visible while preferences
are unsettled.

4     Preference-Sensitivity Methods: Broad and Local
      Priorities

4.1     Three Declared Preference Spaces

The protocol, input hashes, sampling rules, counts, seeds, selection objective and
evaluation criteria were committed before new ranking outcomes were comput-
ed.4 The three closed supports are

                    WF = {w ∈ R3+ : wA + wH + wV = 1},                       (12)
                    WO = {w ∈ WF : wA ≥ wH ≥ wV },                           (13)
                    WL = {w ∈ WF : |wj − wj0 | ≤ 0.10 for all j}.            (14)

WF represents no additional preference restriction within the chosen linear mod-
el. WO represents a solar-access-priority research scenario, not a preference con-
firmed by architects. WL tests proximity to the historical centre with a declared
coordinate half-width. The radius 0.10 is a protocol choice, not an estimated
tolerance of human judgment.
    For each space we generate 20,000 training, 20,000 independent test and
20,000 independent replication weights, totaling 180,000 retained draws. Full-
simplex draws are IID Dirichlet(1, 1, 1), uniform in simplex area. Ordered draws
sort those exchangeable components in descending order, giving uniform area
in the ordered sector. Local draws reject Dirichlet samples outside Eq. 14; ac-
cepted draws are retained in generated order, rather than independently jittered
and renormalized. NumPy PCG64 and float64 are used. Seeds are 2026101801–
03 for training, 2026101811–13 for test and 2026101821–23 for replication, in
full, ordered, local order. Local proposals use batches of 100,000 until 20,000
acceptances are retained.

4.3   Ten-Candidate Selection Objective

For a nonempty shortlist S ⊂ D, define its best achievable utility and absolute
regret:

          B(S, w) = max us (w),           R(S, w) = max ui (w) − B(S, w).      (16)
                      s∈S                             i∈D


Regret lies in [0, 1] under the frozen normalized utility. It is an absolute utility
loss, not a percentage of removed volume, sunlight area or relative regret ratio.
The target finite-scenario problem is

                                min         max R(S, w),                       (17)
                             S⊂D, |S|=10 w∈Wtrain


where Wtrain concatenates 60,000 training draws. We use a greedy heuristic,
called Robust10. Starting with best utility zero, at each step choose the re-
maining candidate yielding the smallest maximum training regret. Exact ties
are resolved by smaller mean regret on the equally weighted training pool, then
natural pair identity. The process always adds ten candidates. It does not solve
Eq. 17 globally, swap earlier choices or exploit evaluation outcomes.

5     Broad and Local Preference Results

5.1     Preference-Specific Rankings Are Not Stable Everywhere

The A/V front contains 12 candidates; the A/H/V front contains 41.5 The
difference matters because an option dominated in area and volume can still offer
valuable mean exposure gain. Restricting all analysis to the two-objective front
would discard such opportunities before weights are considered. Rank statistics
therefore retain the complete 630-candidate universe.
    Historical top-ten membership is much less stable across WF than close to the
historical priorities. Mean test Jaccard overlap is 0.403450 for full, 0.819045 for
ordered and 0.832001 for local weights. Full-space median overlap is 0.428571,
with a fifth percentile of zero. Ordered and local medians are both 0.818182.
These summaries distinguish local stability from global weight insensitivity: a
stable result in a neighborhood does not establish agreement under volume- or
gain-dominant preferences.
    C9/C24 is the only sampled winner under ordered and local weights, but
its full-space winner frequency is 0.4249. C25/C29, historical rank 397, becomes
useful because it minimizes nominal volume; C24/C35 offers the largest H. Full
rank histograms remain archived. These examples explain why broad preference
analysis changes membership rather than simply reordering the historical ten.

bust10 lowers mean regret from 0.010759 to 0.000054. The historical 95th per-
centile is 0.080601, whereas the robust 95th percentile is zero because more than
95% of weights retain a global winner. This does not mean all scenarios have ze-

are frequencies under the declared uniform weight measure, not percentages of

5.3   The Negative Result: Top-Ten Recovery Falls
Opportunity preservation comes with a clear loss on a different outcome. Full-
space top-ten recovery falls from 0.515600 for Historical10 to 0.422480 for Ro-
bust10. Under ordered weights it falls from 0.896180 to 0.443420, and under local
weights from 0.899530 to 0.533730. The robust set is therefore not a more faithful
approximation of every preference-specific top ten. It trades several near-centre
high-ranked options for alternatives that protect other parts of preference space.

7     Integration with Simulation and Geometry Review

7.1     A Decision Workflow with Distinct Evidence Gates

The intended workflow begins with an urban-block model, explicit eligibility and
a fixed intervention rule.7 Fast exposure evaluation produces the complete metric
table. Preference analysis then produces rank distributions and alternative ten-
candidate sets. The architect examines their trade-offs and review status before
requesting additional simulation or developing geometry. Figure 1 separates these
activities.
     A practical first conversation is whether the full simplex is appropriate. If a
project team rules out volume-dominant weights and accepts wA ≥ wH ≥ wV ,
the result already establishes zero fixed-set regret for Historical10 over that con-
tinuous support. If priorities remain open, Robust10 retains more opportunities,
but the team must budget for three previously unreviewed rule candidates and
eight missing translations. If the objective is to offer multiple similar high-benefit
options, top-ten recovery can be more relevant than winner coverage. The in-
terface should expose these choices rather than display one unqualified “best”
list.

8     Discussion, Limitations and Reproducibility

8.1     Architect-Facing Interpretation

The main decision is how to express a project’s sunlight deficit and acceptable
intervention burden.8 Historical10 is a transparent default for review under the
declared score, not a validated professional consensus. Its zero regret under or-
dered and local preferences removes any basis for claiming an improvement on
that measure there. Broad-space Robust10 protects more opportunities when
volume-dominant preferences are genuinely admissible, but its primary-context
mean area benefit is 208.8 versus 309.6 m2 for Historical10. That compromise
need not fit a sunlight-first brief.
    The third round separates ordered priority from a total sunlight emphasis.
Solar70 permits duration-dominant preferences and offers an interpretable op-
tional comparison: review C24/C35 alongside the historical alternatives. Its im-
proved winner coverage is useful, yet does not reverse the failed full replacement
test. Solar80’s greater nominal sunlight weight likewise does not imply greater
area benefit for the entire shortlist. An architect should choose the preference
contract before relying on the resulting menu, then request missing simulation
and geometry evidence.

8.2   Threats to Validity
Preference model. Additive normalized utility assumes compensation between
area, exposure and volume. Bounds and units shape its trade-offs. Uniform sim-
plex area, the ordered restriction, local radius and equal-space mixture are re-
search choices without architect elicitation. Correlated A and H may give sun-
light improvement two related channels of influence. Other utility forms, vetoes
or explicit removal budgets could change selection. The present results do not
establish that any of these weights represent a target professional population.

Optimization and evaluation. Greedy construction is path dependent and is
not globally optimal. Historical-ranking compression makes AV10 and AHV10
useful transparent baselines but not strong optimized subset competitors. The
study lacks a swap heuristic, exact combinatorial benchmark or broader budget
search. The independent LP audit evaluates fixed sets on the chosen supports
only. Monte Carlo confidence intervals describe sampling precision conditional
on fixed performances; they do not cover model uncertainty, selection across
many reported quantities or differences between sites.

Canonical geometry and solar scope. Component-bounding-box unions are not
verified as-built footprints. Shared components, overlaps and OBJ differences can
affect both shade and volume. The single date, 16 time samples, 3 m receiver grid,
0.1 m height, assumed north and 3 h threshold delimit physical interpretation.
Threshold crossings can be sensitive to small exposure changes. Neither annual
performance, indoor daylight, glare, energy outcomes nor statutory compliance
is established.

Evidence coverage. Curated23 and coverage20 remain nonprobability same-
site cohorts. Aggregate agreement can conceal spatial differences and cannot
be transferred automatically to unreviewed alternatives. Archived rule and
translated-design results concern different geometries. Containment removes the
observed outside-footprint failure under tested conditions, but does not prove
structural feasibility, equal removal cost or general correctness of arbitrary
imported meshes. There is no new Honeybee experiment, architect survey,
realized intervention or measured decision-time saving.

Scope of generalization. All 630 alternatives share one rule family and a single
canonical block. Exactly two altered groups excludes many plausible interven-
tions. Property rights, function, heritage, construction sequencing and social im-
pacts are outside the objective vector. A shortlist suitable for this early screening
task may be unsuitable after those constraints are imposed. Replication weights
test numerical sampling stability, not external validity.


8.3     Reproducibility and Release Boundaries

The archived preference rounds retain pre-outcome protocols and input hashes;
scripts and software versions; every retained weight; all 630 rank histograms;
candidate memberships; scenario-level test, replication and grid results; greedy
traces; continuous LP status and witnesses; and tables linked to saved CSVs. His-
torical inputs and manuscripts remain unmodified. This supports re-execution
from fixed aggregate performances, including unfavorable outcomes. The final
manuscript integrates the three existing rounds; preparing it performed no new
ranking, selection, simulation or preference experiment.
    Raw CAD, DXF, SKP and OBJ inputs, receiver coordinates, Honeybee mod-
els, WEA carriers and Radiance intermediates remain restricted. The aggre-
gate and algorithmic package does not confer permission to redistribute them or
fully reproduce site simulations. Anonymous review materials exclude identify-
ing repository links. Any broader release requires rights and anonymity review.
These limitations reduce independent physical reproducibility even though the
new preference experiment is reproducible from the retained table.


9     Conclusion

For a simplified urban solar-deficit case, 630 fixed two-building reductions pro-
vide a finite and interpretable screening universe.9 The A/H/V /N model, histor-
ical Top10 and Pareto alternatives make the initial benefit–intervention trade-off
explicit. Preference analysis reveals its dependence on assumptions: broad-space
Robust10 sharply reduces regret, while Historical10 already has zero regret under
ordered and local priorities and retains more preference-specific top-ten mem-
bers.
    The third round strengthens the limits of that result. None of six con-
texts supports full replacement under the frozen recovery and sunlight-benefit
safeguards. Solar70 and Solar80 nevertheless reveal conditional winner-coverage
gains, accompanied by missing rule reviews and, in some contexts, weaker area
or duration benefits. Hard area floors can also hide full-universe opportunity loss
behind excellent conditional recovery.
    The contribution is application-level decision modeling, an interpretable
preference-sensitive workflow and traceable layered validation. Fast screening,
selective Honeybee review and containment checks support different claims and
must remain distinguishable. They help an architect decide what to inspect
next; they do not supply a new general algorithm, validated expert weights or
a construction-ready answer.
